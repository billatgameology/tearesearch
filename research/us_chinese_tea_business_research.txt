PURPOSE AND SCOPE

This file consolidates the useful business research from the shared conversation into one working brief. Translation requests, television references, personal discussion, and other unrelated material have been removed.

Working assumption: a Chinese founder owns a U.S. LLC that will develop a Chinese-tea business in the United States. The LLC may use a paid U.S.-based manager or operator for local execution. Relevant entity, tax, food, import, employment, licensing, and operating-control questions remain included.

This is a research snapshot, not legal, tax, accounting, food-safety, or investment advice. Many cited businesses are private, so continued operation can be observed but profitability usually cannot. Wage, rent, tariff, tax, licensing, and business-status data should be rechecked before money is committed.


EXECUTIVE CONCLUSION

The opportunity is credible enough to test, but it is not ready for a permanent store.

The strongest entry sequence is:

  English-language content and education
  -> approximately $28 beginner sampler
  -> approximately $45-$60 paid tastings
  -> repeat e-commerce and subscription
  -> wholesale, corporate, and private events
  -> compact appointment studio
  -> public tea house only after the economics are proven

The main finding from prior attempts is not that Chinese tea lacks a U.S. market. Americans have repeatedly paid for Chinese tea, tastings, education, teaware, gifts, and community. The weak model is a large, full-time room supported mainly by ordinary walk-in pots of tea.

The durable businesses tend to combine several revenue streams: paid tea service, packaged leaves, e-commerce, wholesale, teaware, food, classes, events, memberships, subscriptions, corporate gifting, and private experiences. When a physical room closes, the underlying brand often continues online, through wholesale, by appointment, or through education.

The central operating question for this LLC is not merely whether customers like the founder's tea. It is whether a trained local operator can consistently deliver the experience, build trust and community, sell products, protect the brand, and generate repeat purchases. That must be demonstrated in paid manager-led events before a lease is considered.

Best uses of the five leading markets:

  Los Angeles / San Gabriel Valley: best overall demand-validation market.
  San Francisco Bay Area: best premium product, provenance, design, and gifting market.
  Seattle: best recurring education, club, and membership market.
  New York City: best compact high-ticket destination and private-event market.
  Austin: best low-commitment white-space and event-led community experiment.

Recommended testing order: Los Angeles first, Seattle second, Austin third, with premium pop-up and corporate tests in San Francisco and New York. These are tests of different hypotheses, not a simple city ranking.


1. WHAT THE BUSINESS IS REALLY SELLING

"Spreading tea culture" is the mission. It is not yet a customer value proposition. Customers may be buying one or more of the following:

  - Daily caffeine
  - Flavor and sensory discovery
  - Relaxation and structured ritual
  - An alcohol-free social occasion
  - Cultural discovery
  - Expert education
  - Craft, origin, scarcity, and connoisseurship
  - A calm place to work or meet
  - Community and identity
  - Gifting, design, and status
  - A celebration or date-night experience

The research suggests that the product is not merely tea. It is a combination of guided discovery, cultural access, social ritual, physical products, gifts, and community.

Do not combine unrelated tea categories when sizing the opportunity. Traditional Chinese loose-leaf tea, matcha, herbal infusions, bottled tea, sweet iced tea, milk tea, and boba solve different customer needs and have different economics.

Useful adjacent spending comparisons:

  Customer need          Existing destination                 Possible Chinese-tea offer
  ---------------------  -----------------------------------  ------------------------------------------
  Daily caffeine         Coffee, matcha, boba, energy drinks  Fast tea with clear flavor/caffeine cues
  Work or sitting space  Cafes, coffee shops, bookstores      Third place with minimums or membership
  Social night out       Bars, breweries, dessert cafes       Alcohol-free evening tea programming
  Cultural discovery     Museums, tours, classes              Ticketed guided tea experience
  Wellness and calm      Yoga, meditation, spas               Structured tea ritual
  Connoisseurship        Wine, coffee, whisky, chocolate      Provenance and comparative tasting
  Gift and status        Wine, luxury food, design goods      Tea-and-teaware gift sets
  Community              Clubs, classes, fandom spaces        Recurring tea club and member inventory
  Celebration            Brunch, afternoon tea, dining        Group packages with food and tea

Boba and modern tea chains validate willingness to try Asian beverages, convenient formats, sweetness, portability, visual novelty, and everyday-price transactions. They do not validate an hour-long unsweetened session, high-priced leaves, cultural education, or long customer dwell time.

CHAGEE provides a useful modern comparison. It entered Los Angeles in 2025 and expanded rapidly in California. Its portable tea lattes, milk teas, matcha drinks, clear ordering, and visual branding remove much of the friction present in traditional tea. This supports openness to Chinese-branded beverages, but it is weak evidence for the economics of a traditional tea house.


2. U.S. MARKET SIGNALS

Americans consumed almost 85 billion servings of tea in 2021. Approximately 84% was black tea and 15% green tea. This proves broad familiarity with tea as a beverage, not willingness to pay repeatedly for gongfu service, rare Chinese loose leaf, teaware, or education.

Circana data reported by Beverage Industry placed U.S. bag and loose-leaf retail sales at about $1.7 billion in the 52 weeks ending April 20, 2025, up 2.6% in dollars and only 0.9% in volume. The category is real but does not, by itself, justify a lease.

Sources:
  https://www.census.gov/newsroom/stories/hot-tea-month.html
  https://www.bevindustry.com/articles/97646-2025-state-of-the-beverage-industry-tea-market-sees-sales-up-volume-declines

Observed current price signals include:

  - Steep LA tea-ceremony sets: approximately $35-$45.
  - Tea Habitat introductory guided tasting: approximately $50; specialist experiences can reach hundreds of dollars.
  - West China Tea subscription: approximately $30 per month.
  - West China Tea teahouse membership: approximately $130 per month or $1,300 annually.
  - Callisto Tea House private tastings: approximately $35 per person.
  - T Shop in New York: approximately $60 for a one-hour, three-tea small-group session.
  - Tea Drunk private virtual events: minimum approximately $500 per hour before tea and shipping.

These examples support charging for education, access, and an experience rather than treating the product as inexpensive leaves plus hot water.


3. BUSINESS-MODEL PRIORITY

Recommended priority from lowest commitment to highest:

1. Content-led e-commerce
   Use public expertise to create trust. Every piece of content should lead to a sampler, tasting, subscription, gift, or sales conversation. Views alone are not validation.

2. Beginner sampler and repeat product sales
   Use a small, understandable selection. Track product-level gross margin, conversion, reorder interval, and 30/60/90-day repeat purchase.

3. Paid tastings and pop-ups
   Test different value propositions, neighborhoods, ticket prices, and operators without fixed-store exposure.

4. Classes, private events, and corporate experiences
   Monetize expertise and group budgets. These formats can produce higher revenue per hour and per square foot than open-ended tea service.

5. Subscription or membership
   Use only after customers demonstrate recurring behavior. Membership can pay for community space more directly than relying on low-ticket daily visitors.

6. Wholesale, hospitality partnerships, and corporate gifting
   Build repeat volume outside a store and reduce dependence on consumer walk-in traffic.

7. Compact appointment studio
   Screen for qualified customers, explicitly price expert time, and minimize rent, labor, and seating requirements.

8. Public tea house
   Treat this as the final escalation. It must be supported by demonstrated local demand, multiple non-store revenue streams, and a proven local operator.

Models to avoid at the beginning:

  - A large, beautiful Chinese tea house as the first market test
  - Several locations opened close together
  - A mall store dependent on unqualified foot traffic
  - A room supported only by pots of tea
  - A generic manager replacing an untested founder-led experience
  - A lease based on optimistic traffic forecasts
  - Direct importing before repeat-purchase teas are identified
  - Large inventories selected by expert taste rather than customer reorders
  - Treating social reach as equivalent to purchases
  - Treating boba demand as proof of traditional-tea demand


4. ILLUSTRATIVE UNIT ECONOMICS

The conversation modeled a manager-operated tea house with these analyst assumptions:

  Startup capital:             $500,000
  Monthly fixed costs:          $65,000
  Contribution margin:              68%
  Average transaction:             $26

Illustrative operating break-even:

  Monthly sales:                about $95,600
  Monthly transactions:         about 3,676
  Daily transactions, 30 days:  about 123
  Daily transactions, 26 days:  about 141

At sales 30% below break-even, this model loses approximately $19,500 per month before debt service or investor return. A weak store could therefore consume more than $200,000 a year while "learning."

The modeled hybrid of tastings, pop-ups, and e-commerce reached illustrative break-even around $21,800 per month while preserving the ability to change the city, offer, price, and format.

These are scenarios, not forecasts. Every proposed location needs current manager compensation, payroll burden, rent, common-area charges, utilities, insurance, permits, build-out, waste, payment fees, customer-acquisition cost, and product gross margin.

The founder's labor must never be modeled as free. The economics must work with a properly paid local operator.


5. U.S. LLC OWNERSHIP, GOVERNANCE, AND OPERATING CONTROLS

Working entity assumption:

  - A Chinese founder owns the U.S. LLC and controls strategy.
  - The LLC owns the brand, intellectual property, domains, websites, customer data, social accounts, recipes/protocols, inventory, supplier contracts, bank accounts, and POS accounts.
  - A paid U.S.-based manager or event operator handles delegated local execution.
  - Employment or contractor classification must match the actual work and applicable law.
  - A manager does not automatically receive equity. Any equity, profit interest, or long-term incentive should be a deliberate written decision with vesting, repurchase, confidentiality, and IP terms.

Entity and tax points retained from the research:

  - U.S. rules allow a U.S. LLC to have a foreign owner.
  - A foreign-owned U.S. disregarded entity may have specialized federal reporting, including a pro forma Form 1120 and Form 5472 for reportable transactions.
  - Failure to file Form 5472 properly can trigger a substantial initial penalty; the research cited $25,000.
  - The correct federal classification, state filings, beneficial-ownership reporting status, sales-tax registration, payroll setup, and related-party transaction treatment must be reviewed for the actual entity and year.
  - Use a cross-border CPA before capital, inventory, management fees, reimbursements, loans, or intellectual property move between the founder and the LLC.

Primary tax sources:
  https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i5472
  https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1120
  https://www.irs.gov/publications/p519

Recommended operating controls:

  - Written operating agreement and authority matrix
  - Separate business banking and bookkeeping
  - Founder or company-level visibility into bank, POS, payroll, inventory, and e-commerce systems
  - Dual approval above defined spending and contracting thresholds
  - No commingling of founder, manager, and company funds
  - Company-owned customer list and social-media credentials
  - Role-based access and immediate offboarding procedures
  - Purchase orders, receiving records, inventory counts, shrinkage reporting, and cash controls
  - Written supplier, event-venue, wholesale, and manager agreements
  - Background and reference checks for anyone controlling money, inventory, customer data, or keys
  - Product liability, general liability, workers' compensation, property, cyber, and event coverage as applicable
  - State and local entity registration, sales-tax, health, food-handler, occupancy, signage, and event permits as applicable
  - A local manager trial through paid events before any permanent-space authority is granted

The decisive operator test is whether a local manager can independently:

  - Teach Chinese tea accurately and accessibly
  - Deliver hospitality and tell the brand story
  - Lead a room and build community
  - Convert participants into product customers
  - Produce repeat attendance and purchases
  - Protect inventory, money, customer data, and brand standards
  - Report operational results reliably


6. SUPPLY CHAIN, IMPORTING, AND PRODUCT COMPLIANCE

Recommended first-six-to-twelve-month approach: buy through a qualified U.S. tea importer or distributor. Direct farm sourcing should begin only after winning teas and dependable reorder volumes are known.

Direct importing may involve:

  - FDA foreign-facility registration
  - Prior notice for imported food
  - Foreign Supplier Verification Program responsibility
  - Ingredient, net-weight, identity, business, nutrition-exemption, and other labeling questions
  - Customs classification and entry records
  - Country-of-origin and China-specific tariff measures
  - Supplier verification, traceability, contaminant/pesticide testing, and safety records
  - Freight, customs brokerage, insurance, currency, delay, and port risk

Base tariff rates on tea may be low, but product form, packaging, teaware, and applicable Chapter 99 measures require shipment-level verification.

Primary starting sources:
  https://www.fda.gov/food/food-imports-exports/importing-food-products-united-states
  https://www.fda.gov/food/food-safety-modernization-act-fsma/fsma-final-rule-foreign-supplier-verification-programs-fsvp-importers-food-humans-and-animals
  https://www.fda.gov/industry/fda-import-process/prior-notice-imported-foods
  https://www.fda.gov/food/food-industry/how-start-food-business
  https://hts.usitc.gov/

Why this matters:

  - Denong Tea temporarily suspended U.S. sales in 2026 when freight carriers stopped accepting food shipments and U.S. inventory ran low.
  - West China Tea publicly described tariff exposure because its tea and much of its teaware come from China.
  - Cultural authenticity and direct sourcing create differentiation but can also create a single point of failure.

Minimum supply protections before scale:

  - At least two viable U.S. supply arrangements
  - Safety stock based on actual lead-time variance
  - Product-level landed-cost and gross-margin model
  - Backup substitutions approved before a disruption
  - Written specifications and quality acceptance criteria
  - Lot traceability and recall procedure
  - Product-liability insurance
  - Direct import only after repeat-purchase evidence justifies the complexity and working capital

13. PRIOR ATTEMPTS AND WHAT THEY TEACH

13.1 Seven Cups: strongest long-term precedent

Seven Cups began at a Tucson farmers' market, built a local base, and opened its tea house in 2004. It sources from more than 30 Chinese producers and combines the physical room with e-commerce, wholesale, and brokerage. The founders reported sales to customers in more than 100 countries; the company remained active with current teas in 2026.

Why it appears durable: expertise and supply first; low-cost local selling; community before the lease; physical room only after demand; national and B2B channels afterward. The room acts as cultural center, showroom, educational space, and community hub rather than carrying the company alone.

Lesson: build demand before the storefront. A non-coastal city can work when a concentrated paying community is created first.

Sources:
  https://sevencups.com/about-us/
  https://sevencups.com/2022/01/happy-chinese-new-year-from-seven-cups/

13.2 West China Tea: real community, difficult economics

West China Tea began with free service, festivals, meetups, and leaf sales from another business. Its first room in 2014 was about 200 square feet. It expanded within two years to approximately 3,000 square feet with classes, events, late-night parties, retail, and community programming.

The larger space created rent, inventory, hiring, and infrastructure pressure. The pandemic eliminated in-person service while fixed space costs remained, requiring a rapid e-commerce pivot. The company later had to relocate because of I-35 expansion and reopened in May 2025 after an eleven-month interruption. It remains active with a physical room, e-commerce, subscription, membership, events, classes, rentals, and long hours.

Lesson: a successful community does not make a large room safe. Do not copy the jump from 200 to 3,000 square feet before recurring economics are proven.

Sources:
  https://voyageaustin.com/interview/hidden-gems-meet-so-han-fan-of-west-china-tea/
  https://cbsaustin.com/news/local/90-day-tariff-pause-brings-temporary-relief-to-austin-tea-house-preparing-to-re-open
  https://westchinatea.com/

13.3 Tea Habitat: customer-channel mismatch corrected by appointments

Tea Habitat built a specialist business around rare Chinese tea, especially single-tree dancong. Serious national customers bought online, while casual walk-ins often wanted ordinary iced tea. The founder's expertise and hosting were central to the product.

The business evolved into online, wholesale, and an appointment-based Alhambra tasting room charging explicitly for guided expertise.

Lesson: high foot traffic can be low qualified traffic. Do not pay premium rent to attract a $6 iced-drink customer when the actual offer is a $50 expert session and a high-value take-home purchase.

Sources:
  https://teahabitat.com/blogs/art-of-tea/at-tea-habitat-tea-connoisseurship-is-taken-to-the-extreme
  https://teahabitat.com/collections/tea-tasting

13.4 Te Company: online and wholesale before tiny rooms

Te Company was founded in 2012 around Taiwanese tea from small farms. It first built online and restaurant-wholesale demand, then opened a West Village room in 2015. It now uses two deliberately small New York rooms with waitlist management, tea service, takeaway, leaves, teaware, gift sets, subscriptions, snacks, corporate gifting, and events.

Lesson: develop product, suppliers, restaurant customers, online customers, and credibility first. A first room can be small and demand-constrained rather than large and dependent on constant foot traffic. Snacks increase ticket size and accessibility without requiring a full restaurant concept.

Sources:
  https://tecompanytea.com/pages/our-story
  https://tecompanytea.com/pages/tea-shop

13.5 Steep LA: multiple dayparts and broader hospitality

Steep LA expanded traditional daytime ceremony sets into food, dessert, retail, tea cocktails, nonalcoholic evening drinks, subscriptions, and events. It monetizes the same space across several dayparts.

Lesson: food and evening social use can make Chinese tea more accessible and improve space utilization. The tradeoff is restaurant/bar complexity, permits, labor, training, and brand drift.

Source:
  https://steepla.com/pages/steep-after-dark

13.6 Ching Ching Cha: loved concept versus rent

Ching Ching Cha operated in Georgetown from 1998 to January 2023. Its quiet, slow experience encouraged customers to stay for hours. A reported 48% rent increase made preserving that format impossible without changing its nature, so the owner moved to a smaller Dupont Circle space. The current business continues as tea shop, pottery gallery, and intimate tea space without food.

Lesson: the same long, calm dwell time that creates cultural value caps revenue per seat. The lease determines whether the concept can preserve its character. Charge for time/experience and avoid occupancy costs that require high turnover.

Sources:
  https://georgetownvoice.com/2023/02/03/for-authenticity-ching-ching-cha-stay-it-had-to-leave/
  https://www.chingchingcha.com/

13.7 Tea Drunk: room closed, knowledge business survived

Tea Drunk's East Village room ended after about eight years when its lease expired in August 2021. The company retained the valuable assets: expertise, reputation, audience, education, supplier access, inventory, and community. It now sells premium tea, clubs/classes, guided tastings, and private in-person or virtual events.

Lesson: the underlying business may be expertise plus product; the tea house is only one delivery mechanism.

Sources:
  https://evgrieve.com/2021/08/last-weekend-for-tea-drunk-on-7th-street.html
  https://www.teadrunk.com/

13.8 Denong Tea: storefront retreat and supply interruption

Denong built a premium pu-erh operation and an elaborate Pasadena space. It ended the original physical retail operation in April 2024 while initially maintaining online service. In May 2026 it temporarily suspended U.S. sales because carriers had stopped accepting relevant food shipments, U.S. inventory was nearly exhausted, and replenishment was unavailable.

The 2026 supply cause is confirmed. The public evidence is insufficient to assign a specific financial cause to the 2024 room closure.

Lesson: a narrow premium category, high education requirement, inventory concentration, and one international replenishment route create fragility. Use multiple routes, safety stock, and initially a qualified U.S. importer.

Sources:
  https://www.instagram.com/p/C7Xt-2jPxqG/
  https://www.denongtea.com/pages/temporary-pause-in-u-s-shipments

13.9 Other physical-store retreats

  - The Green Teahouse closed its Westfarms Mall location in August 2025 after 16 years and continued online. The owner described the move as a shift toward growing online business; detailed store economics were not disclosed.
    https://www.thegreenteahouse.com/

  - Samovar closed physical lounges during the pandemic and continued selling tea and classes online; some former spaces became a different restaurant concept.
    https://missionlocal.org/2020/08/samovar-tea-locations-go-into-covid-19-hibernation-as-company-emerges-online-with-classes/

  - Tao of Tea has operated since 1997 while opening and retiring different locations and formats. Its durable core includes the Belmont tea house, a separate Leaf Room, e-commerce, and wholesale.
    https://taooftea.com/teahouses/history/

Combined lesson: durable brands prune physical channels while preserving e-commerce, wholesale, education, or a smaller core room.

13.10 Large specialty-tea chains

Teavana: Starbucks announced in 2017 that all 379 primarily mall-based stores would close after persistent underperformance that merchandising and redesigns did not reverse. This is the clearest documented failure of scaled physical specialty-tea retail.
Source: https://investor.starbucks.com/news/financial-releases/news-details/2017/Starbucks-Reports-Record-Q3-Financial-and-Operating-Results-Company-Announces-Strategic-Actions-to-Advance-Growth-Agenda-and-Increase-Returns/default.aspx

American Tea Room: operated three Southern California lounge/retail locations and abruptly closed all three in November 2017 while continuing online. Detailed unit economics were not disclosed. It is reasonable, but unconfirmed, to infer that multiple expensive rooms multiplied fixed-cost exposure before repeatable unit economics were proven.
Source: https://la.eater.com/2017/11/29/16715288/american-tea-room-closed-shutter-all-locations

DAVIDsTEA: entered creditor protection in 2020, closed all 42 U.S. stores and many Canadian stores, and shifted toward e-commerce and wholesale. It later returned to profitability with a smaller, mixed-channel system and selective store additions. This suggests stores were not inherently useless; the oversized leased network was the problem.
Source: https://globalnews.ca/news/7160002/davidstea-canadian-store-closures-coronavirus/

T2 Tea: exited the United States and United Kingdom in 2023 and later its remaining Singapore stores, concentrating closer to its Australian base. Attractive branding and international recognition did not make a broad physical network automatically sustainable.
Source: https://insideretail.com.au/stores/openings-closings/t2-tea-to-exit-singapore-closing-three-remaining-stores-202603


14. REPEATED FAILURE MECHANISMS

1. Slow experience versus expensive rent
   Tea's intended calm, long session creates low table turnover and a ceiling on revenue per seat. Price the experience, constrain the footprint, use reservations/minimums, or fund community space through membership and other channels.

2. Store before market
   Strong survivors used farmers' markets, free community service, events, online, or wholesale first. Treat a lease as the result of proven demand, not the tool used to create it.

3. Unqualified foot traffic
   Boba, mall, tourist, or cafe traffic is not automatically demand for traditional tea. Measure paid conversion, take-home purchases, and repeat behavior by neighborhood and offer.

4. One revenue stream
   A room supported only by pots of tea is the most fragile format. Durable examples combine service, products, online, wholesale, teaware, food, education, events, membership, subscription, and gifting.

5. Founder/operator dependence
   The host's knowledge, taste, cultural interpretation, hospitality, and personality may be the core product. A generic retail manager is not an automatic substitute.

6. China-dependent inventory concentration
   Direct sourcing creates differentiation but also freight, customs, tariff, compliance, currency, and geopolitical exposure. Use qualified import support, more than one route, safety stock, and reorder evidence.

7. Community value versus commercial value
   Loyal communities often want accessible prices, long stays, and unhurried atmosphere. Decide who pays for the space: members, product buyers, events, corporate clients, or daily visitors.

8. Premature scale
   The jump from a small proven format to a large room or network multiplies rent, staffing, inventory, infrastructure, and management risk faster than it multiplies demand.


15. MARKET-SPECIFIC TESTS

Los Angeles / SGV
  - Run paired SGV and central-LA events.
  - Measure authenticity acceptance, experience willingness-to-pay, take-home conversion, and repeat buying.
  - Do not combine the submarket results.

San Francisco
  - Corporate team tastings
  - Premium gift sets
  - Tea-and-ceramics events
  - Small private sessions
  - Seasonal allocation or subscription
  - Avoid low-priced walk-in service as the first model

Seattle
  - Four- or six-week educational series
  - Monthly membership
  - Tea flights modeled after coffee cupping
  - Beginner-to-enthusiast progression
  - Evening tea club
  - Optimize for recurring participation, not first-event curiosity

New York
  - Flushing: product authenticity, comparative tasting, retail purchase
  - Manhattan/Brooklyn: $60-$100 destination experience and corporate/private events
  - Analyze the two offers separately

Austin
  - Pop-ups and late-night alcohol-free events
  - Tea and music or wellness collaborations
  - Membership and local pickup
  - Hotel, restaurant, and cafe wholesale
  - Climate-suitable cold tea entry products
  - Do not sign a lease based only on event crowds; measure return and post-event product sales


16. VALIDATION METRICS AND LEASE GATES

The conversation contained two progressively stricter sets of proposed gates. Use the stricter requirement where they overlap.

Minimum evidence before considering a permanent space:

  - At least 20 paid events in one metro
  - Stable attendance, pricing, and contribution margin
  - At least eight events led entirely by the prospective local manager
  - At least 70% paid capacity across the most recent six comparable events
  - At least 300 unique paying local customers
  - At least 1,000 qualified local email/SMS contacts
  - Approximately 30%-35% 90-day repeat purchase, or comparable subscription retention
  - Evidence that tasting customers subsequently buy tea; not only one-time event attendance
  - At least 65% product gross margin
  - Customer-acquisition-cost payback within 90 days
  - Multiple functioning revenue streams: events, leaves, e-commerce, gifting, membership, or wholesale
  - At least two viable U.S. supply arrangements
  - A compact-room model that pays a real market salary to the manager
  - 12 months of runway remaining after build-out plus 20% contingency
  - Survival for 12 months at revenue 30% below plan
  - A downside plan covering tariff increase, relocation delay, and supply interruption
  - At least $20,000 monthly non-store revenue before considering a tasting studio
  - At least $35,000 monthly non-store revenue before considering staffed retail

Suggested metrics by funnel stage:

Awareness
  Qualified local subscribers, event-page conversion, source/channel, content-to-offer click-through.

Acquisition
  Paid customers, customer-acquisition cost, preorder conversion, event fill rate, no-show/refund rate.

Experience
  Ticket revenue, contribution per event/hour/seat, manager score, post-event product conversion, referrals.

Product
  Units by SKU, landed cost, gross margin, 30/60/90-day reorder, inventory turns, shrinkage, return/refund.

Retention
  Repeat event attendance, repeat purchase, subscription retention, membership usage and churn.

B2B
  Qualified conversations, samples, conversion, average order, reorder interval, gross margin, concentration.

Operations
  Labor hours, prep/cleanup time, service capacity, error rate, cash variance, inventory variance, customer issues.


17. PRACTICAL 90-DAY VALIDATION PROGRAM

Weeks 1-2: foundation

  - Select one beginner customer and one primary promise.
  - Form/check the LLC, banking, accounting, insurance, sales-tax, and local event requirements.
  - Choose a qualified U.S. importer/distributor and a small initial range.
  - Build SKU-level landed cost and margin.
  - Define brand, content pillars, event protocol, and manager scorecard.
  - Recruit and train the proposed local operator.

Weeks 3-4: offer creation

  - Create the beginner sampler, tasting ticket, follow-up product offer, and gift option.
  - Build a simple landing page, local email/SMS capture, POS, and measurement plan.
  - Run private rehearsals led by the manager; document timing and service standards.

Weeks 5-8: paid tests

  - Run several paid tastings in the first metro and at least two distinct neighborhood contexts.
  - Require the manager to lead some sessions independently.
  - Test at least two messages: cultural/craft education and social/wellness occasion.
  - Offer take-home product at every event.
  - Start small B2B outreach to cafes, restaurants, hotels, employers, and event planners.

Weeks 9-12: retention and decision

  - Measure repeat purchase and reattendance.
  - Run a second event format and test a simple subscription/membership expression of interest.
  - Compare manager-led results with founder-led or jointly led results.
  - Calculate contribution by channel and the cash required to repeat.
  - Decide whether to continue, change audience/offer/city, or stop.

The original conversation referenced an illustrative $49,000 90-day validation budget. Rebuild that budget with current vendor quotes before use; do not treat it as an approved spending plan.


18. KILL OR PIVOT CRITERIA

Do not move toward a lease if several of the following remain true after disciplined testing:

  - Events fill only through discounts, free attendance, or the founder's personal network
  - Customers enjoy the event but do not buy leaves or return
  - The local operator cannot reproduce the experience without the founder
  - Product gross margin is below target after actual landed costs and waste
  - Customer-acquisition payback is too slow
  - Demand is too geographically scattered for one room
  - The concept depends on long stays without a credible way to monetize time/space
  - Direct sourcing complexity arrives before dependable reorder volume
  - A single supplier, importer, route, or manager is a critical point of failure
  - The downside case requires unpaid founder labor or repeated emergency capital
  - A location works only under optimistic traffic, rent, or labor assumptions

A failed store test does not necessarily end the business. The evidence supports viable alternatives: content-and-commerce brand, online specialist, wholesaler, appointment tasting room, educational company, corporate/private event provider, membership club, or hybrid hospitality concept.


19. DECISION SUMMARY

Recommended thesis:

  Build a U.S. Chinese-tea company before building a tea house.

The U.S. LLC should first own and prove the customer relationship, product selection, operating system, local manager capability, and multiple revenue channels. A permanent room should be acquired only when it is the lowest-risk way to serve already demonstrated demand.

Best combined precedent model:

  - Seven Cups: community and product demand before the lease
  - Te Company: online and wholesale before tiny physical rooms
  - Tea Habitat: advanced experiences by appointment and explicitly paid
  - Tea Drunk: expertise monetized through education, club, and private events
  - Steep LA: accessible entry products and multiple dayparts
  - West China Tea: authentic community, without copying premature large-space expansion

The biggest opportunity is not simply another tea shop. It is finding which existing American spending habit--coffee ritual, boba convenience, wine-style tasting, wellness, nightlife, gifting, cultural education, or membership--is the easiest doorway into repeat Chinese-tea purchases.

The final go/no-go question is:

  Can the U.S. LLC acquire repeat local customers and can its trained local operator deliver the experience profitably and consistently without depending on a permanent storefront?

Until the answer is yes, the lease is premature.
