# New York City Chinese Tea Market Research

**Research date:** August 28, 2026  
**Business assumption:** A Chinese-founded U.S. LLC selling premium Chinese tea, guided tea experiences, take-home tea, gifting and related services.  
**Geography:** New York City, with detailed comparisons of Flushing North/core, Flushing South/Murray Hill, Manhattan Chinatown/Lower East Side, East Village/NoHo, Koreatown/Herald Square, Midtown East, Long Island City, Downtown Brooklyn, Williamsburg, Greenpoint, Sunset Park and Jackson Heights/Elmhurst.  
**Method:** Public-source desk research. Current operator status, public prices, posted hours, wages and visible asking rents were checked on August 28, 2026 unless another date is stated.

> **Decision status:** New York has enough evidence to justify a paid, multi-neighborhood validation program. It does not yet have enough evidence to justify a permanent lease.

## 1. Executive decision

### Recommendation

Run New York as a **four-lane validation market**:

1. **Chinese-community/product lane:** start with Flushing North/core and Sunset Park. Test whether tea expertise converts into paid guided use, take-home attachment and reorder behavior in places where the cultural fit is strongest.
2. **Downtown destination lane:** use Manhattan Chinatown/LES and East Village/NoHo for visitor, date-night and enthusiast sessions through host venues. These districts provide strong discovery and specialist context, but asking rents and incumbent density make them poor lease-first choices.
3. **Workplace/private-event lane:** sell portable tea programs into Koreatown/Herald, Midtown East, Long Island City and Downtown Brooklyn. Their workplace-job and access signals are more useful for B2B outreach than for assuming all-day walk-in traffic.
4. **Brooklyn lifestyle lane:** use Williamsburg or Greenpoint for premium design/education tests, while treating Kettl, Bellocq and Puerh Brooklyn as evidence that the customer already has strong alternatives.

Do **not** begin with a conventional walk-in tearoom. New York already offers a $20 guided tasting, a $60 personal tea journey, $68-$78 classes, free acquisition tastings, self-guided gongfu, premium Japanese education, $90-$120 luxury tastings, prepared-drink chains, subscriptions, wholesale and corporate programs. A new operator needs an economic system more specific than “excellent tea in a calm room.”

### Best current concept hypothesis

A **reservation-led Chinese tea studio without a long lease**, paired with a portable corporate/cultural-events business and take-home product, best fits the evidence.

The first offer ladder should test distinct jobs rather than one complicated menu:

| Offer | Current NYC evidence | Proposed role |
|---|---:|---|
| Fast unsweetened tea or short comparative flight | Prepared specialty drinks commonly occupy roughly the mid-single digits to low teens; Brooklyn Tea lists a self-guided five-pour flight at **$25** | Accessible discovery and event throughput |
| Beginner guided tasting | Fang posts **from $20 for 45 minutes** | Low-friction paid trial; must be clearly more than a retail sample |
| Personal guided journey | T Shop posts **$60 per person for about one hour and three teas** | Core reservation benchmark |
| Structured class | Té Company lists **$68-$78 for 90 minutes**; Kettl lists **$70-$90** education products | Education, community and customer acquisition |
| Luxury/collector tasting | Bellocq lists **$90-$120** tastings | Upper-end presentation benchmark, not the default mass offer |
| Private/corporate session | Mansa displays a **$60 class product and a $650 event minimum**; the public page does not state a simple per-person event rate | Portable revenue and employer acquisition |
| Product, subscription and gifting | Current operators visibly sell tea, teaware, subscriptions, corporate gifts and wholesale | Repeat revenue beyond seat-hours |

These are observed competitor prices, not proof of the new company’s optimal price or margin. A pilot must measure labor minutes, seat utilization, product attachment, acquisition cost and contribution profit directly.

### What the online evidence supports

- **Paid education exists at several levels.** Fang, T Shop, Té Company, Kettl and Bellocq publish bookable products from $20 to $120.
- **Traditional tea can support multiple revenue layers.** Current operators combine room service, retail/e-commerce, teaware, subscriptions, classes, wholesale, corporate events, catering or consulting.
- **The category is both credible and crowded.** Flushing has Fang and Ten Ren plus modern tea chains; downtown Manhattan has T Shop, Té Company, CHICHA San Chen, Tea Dealers and other specialists; North Brooklyn has Puerh Brooklyn, Kettl and Bellocq.
- **Portable demand is demonstrated.** T Shop, Té Company, Mansa and CHICHA publish corporate, restaurant, hotel, catering or private-event offers.
- **Citywide visitor and meeting channels are large.** New York City Tourism + Conventions reported 65 million visitors, $55.6 billion in direct visitor spending, 12.6 million business travelers and 1,515 booked meetings/events producing almost 345,000 definite room nights in 2025. These are channel indicators, not a tea-demand forecast. ([2025 tourism report](https://www.business.nyctourism.com/press-media/press-releases/NYC-Tourism-Annual-Report-March-2026?pubDate=20260419))
- **The fixed-cost hurdle is material.** NYC’s general minimum wage is **$17.00 per hour from January 1, 2026**, while the live rent examples in this report range from roughly $43 to more than $150 per square foot per year for very different spaces. ([NYC wage rules](https://nyc-business.nyc.gov/nycbusiness/description/wage-regulations-in-new-york-state?mod=article_inline))

### What remains unproven

- Whether customers who attend an event will buy unsweetened Chinese loose-leaf tea and reorder it.
- Whether the concept can be legible to beginners while still earning trust from knowledgeable tea customers.
- Which neighborhood produces the best fully loaded customer-acquisition cost after venue, permits, local travel and labor.
- Whether Chinese-community demand, visitor/occasion demand or employer demand is most repeatable.
- Whether a trained host can reproduce the experience without founder dependence.
- Whether any specific storefront can support its total occupancy, build-out, staffing and compliance burden.
- Whether the durable business should center on events, product/e-commerce, wholesale, a small appointment studio or a hybrid.

## 2. Evidence rules used in this report

The report keeps four kinds of statements separate:

- **Measured fact:** a published government, operator or institutional number.
- **Observed market evidence:** a current price, operating format, location, opening, closure or practice visible on a primary site or corroborated by reputable reporting.
- **Inference:** a conclusion drawn from multiple observations; it is labeled.
- **Proposed test:** a target or experiment for this company; it is not presented as an industry benchmark.

Private businesses generally do not publish store-level revenue, profit, rent burden, traffic, acquisition cost or retention. In the competitor tables, **visible revenue layers** means products and services publicly offered, not the amount or profitability of those streams. Continued operation is evidence of durability, not proof of profit.

Location scores are screening tools, not forecasts. ZCTAs are postal approximations, workplace jobs are not foot traffic, Business Patterns categories are broader than tea, and one asking-rent listing is not a market rent survey.

## 3. Why New York is worth testing

### 3.1 A real paid tasting ladder already exists

The specialist market shows that customers can encounter Chinese or adjacent tea in at least six ways:

| Customer job | Current observable evidence | Competitive meaning |
|---|---|---|
| “Let me try serious tea cheaply” | Fang: **from $20**, 45 minutes | A beginner offer cannot assume $50+ is the entry point |
| “Guide me personally” | T Shop Tea Journey: **$60 per person**, about one hour, three teas, parties of one to four | Personal curation is already monetized and clearly explained |
| “Teach me a reusable skill” | Té Company: **$68-$78**, 90 minutes | Technique and curriculum can justify a higher ticket |
| “Give me a luxury ritual” | Bellocq: **$90-$120** focused tastings | Design, scarcity and setting support a premium tier |
| “Let me brew or gather on my own” | Puerh Brooklyn offers self-guided gongfu and a dedicated downstairs tearoom | Host labor does not have to scale one-for-one with room time |
| “Bring tea to my team or venue” | Mansa lists a **$650 minimum**; Té, T Shop and CHICHA publish corporate/catering formats | Portable B2B is an established customer job |

Sources: [Fang](https://www.fangtea.com/book-online), [T Shop](https://www.tshopny.com/tea-journey), [Té Company](https://tecompanytea.com/pages/classes-and-events), [Bellocq](https://www.bellocqtea.com/collections/tea-tastings-nyc), [Puerh Brooklyn](https://www.puerhbrooklyn.com/tearoom.html), [Mansa](https://mansatea.com/products/tea-class), [CHICHA catering](https://chichasanchenny.com/catering).

**Inference:** the whitespace is not “New York has no tea tastings.” The opening is a dependable, beginner-safe Chinese regional curriculum with better conversion from experience to product, repeat use and private events.

### 3.2 Current operators prove diversified formats, not one winning room model

- T Shop combines in-room service, guided journeys, retail/e-commerce, subscription, wholesale, gifts and private company tastings. Its wholesale page names restaurant service and company programs. ([T Shop wholesale/private](https://www.tshopny.com/wholesale/))
- Té Company combines two small tearooms, snacks, classes, retail/e-commerce, subscriptions and corporate catering. Its corporate page displays clients across technology, consulting, media and finance. ([Té corporate catering](https://tecompanytea.com/pages/corporate-catering))
- Mansa operates private/virtual classes, pop-ups, product and hospitality consulting instead of relying on a conventional public room. ([Mansa visit and consulting](https://mansatea.com/pages/visit))
- CHICHA combines high-throughput prepared drinks with complimentary Lishan oolong sampling, a reservation ceremony, store events and on-site tasting catering. ([CHICHA NYC](https://chichasanchenny.com/))
- Kettl combines café throughput, a six-seat tasting counter, classes, e-commerce, wholesale and a non-walk-in studio used for chef collaborations. ([Kettl locations](https://kettl.co/pages/locations))

**Inference:** a permanent room is one distribution option, not the business itself. The pilot should find the repeatable revenue engine before selecting real estate.

### 3.3 New York contains four different demand systems

**Flushing and Sunset Park:** strongest Chinese-community proxies in the selected ZCTAs, dense restaurant ecosystems and legacy/current tea brands. These are the clearest places to test product credibility and resident repeat purchase, but lower median household income signals mean premium pricing cannot be assumed.

**Chinatown/LES and East Village/NoHo:** visitor discovery, nightlife, culture and a dense specialist set. These districts are strong for ticketed experiences and partnerships but carry high asking rents and many substitutes.

**Koreatown/Herald, Midtown East, LIC and Downtown Brooklyn:** large workplace-job counts, strong access and business/event systems. They are attractive for private programs and gifting; 2023 workplace jobs do not prove that office workers are present every day.

**Williamsburg and Greenpoint:** affluent, design-oriented and experience-friendly, but with relatively weak Chinese-community proxies and unusually capable tea incumbents.

These are market-structure inferences. Exact demographic, jobs, business-count, access, rent and scoring inputs appear in Appendix A.

### 3.4 Visitor scale creates a channel, not guaranteed customers

New York City Tourism + Conventions reported **65 million visitors in 2025**, including **52.4 million domestic** and **12.5 million international** visitors. It reported **$55.6 billion in direct spending**, **12.6 million business travelers**, 84.2% hotel occupancy and 38.1 million room nights sold. Its 2026 forecast was **66.3 million visitors** and **12.8 million business travelers**. ([official March 2026 report](https://www.business.nyctourism.com/press-media/press-releases/NYC-Tourism-Annual-Report-March-2026?pubDate=20260419))

Those figures support outreach to hotels, destination managers, convention planners and cultural venues. They do not show what fraction wants Chinese tea, will travel to a specific neighborhood or will pay a specific ticket.

### 3.5 Institutions and current programming create partnership routes

- The Museum of Chinese in America rents classrooms and event rooms at 215 Centre Street; published rental fees generally begin at **$1,000 per hour per space** with a two-hour minimum, and outside catering may be approved. That price makes MOCA a better co-produced or sponsored channel than a casual low-ticket host. ([MOCA event rentals](https://www.mocanyc.org/get-involved/event-rentals/))
- MOCA’s February 2026 Lunar New Year mixer included Té Company among its food/tea partners, directly demonstrating institutional placement for a specialist tea brand. ([MOCA event](https://www.mocanyc.org/event/lunar-new-year-mixer-horse/))
- Flushing Town Hall runs year-round cultural, school and rental programming. ([Flushing Town Hall](https://www.flushingtownhall.org/))
- Queens Botanical Garden in Flushing rents meeting, celebration and corporate-event spaces and explicitly promotes garden “Par-Teas.” ([QBG special events](https://queensbotanical.org/specialevents/))
- Downtown Brooklyn Partnership manages public plazas and accepts event, market and pop-up applications; its form says the plazas can reach residents and office workers in the borough’s central business district. ([DBP plaza application](https://downtownbrooklyn.typeform.com/to/gRkunY5C))
- The Queens Night Market’s current 2026 calendar shows an operating food-vendor platform at Flushing Meadows Corona Park. It is more suitable for a fast service/product test than a quiet 60-minute tasting. ([Queens Night Market vendors](https://queensnightmarket.com/vendors))

### 4.3 What pricing actually says

The observed price ladder represents different customer jobs, not one “market price”:

1. **Free or low-cost acquisition:** CHICHA’s complimentary tasting and Fang’s $20 entry experience.
2. **Prepared consumption:** modern tea, café tea, desserts and afternoon tea compete on convenience and social use.
3. **Guided education:** T Shop, Té, Kettl and Brooklyn Tea charge for curation, comparison and skill.
4. **Luxury ritual:** Bellocq charges for setting, focus and premium positioning.
5. **Private portability:** Mansa, Té, T Shop and CHICHA take service to groups, companies or venues.

**Inference:** the company should publish duration, group size, tea count, learning outcome and take-home value. “Tea ceremony” alone does not make the product comparable or understandable.

A neighborhood can fit an appointment studio and perform poorly as a spontaneous flagship. The following scores compare structural fit, not market share, sales or site viability. Exact frontage, permitted use, utilities, pedestrian path, lease terms and partner access can reverse the result.

### 5.3 Decision interpretation

1. **Flushing North/core — first cultural/product test.** Fang’s $20 tasting is a direct price and service benchmark; Ten Ren and modern chains prove tea shopping and prepared-drink use. Run a differentiated regional curriculum and measure leaf attachment, not generic category interest.
2. **Flushing South/Murray Hill — resident repeat-purchase test.** The selected ZIP has the highest Chinese-community proxy but low purchasing-power and daytime indices. Use an affordable host venue/community partnership; do not import Manhattan pricing without testing.
3. **Manhattan Chinatown/LES — first downtown visitor/culture test.** Strong cultural signals, direct tea brands, MOCA and visitor discovery make it useful for ticketed collaborations. High competition and $86-per-square-foot live restaurant-space evidence argue against a first lease.
4. **East Village/NoHo — first enthusiast/lifestyle test.** T Shop, Té, Physical Graffitea, CHICHA nearby and former Tea Drunk/29B history make this the most legible specialist ecosystem. It is a good place to test differentiated content and a poor place to assume novelty.
5. **Koreatown/Herald — strongest B2B and flagship index, not first room.** The score is driven by 236,873 workplace jobs in ZIP 10001, high income/education, food-service density and exceptional access. Jin Yun Fu provides cultural context. Start with employer, hotel and event buyers.
6. **Midtown East — private-event and gifting lane.** Very high income and workplace signals, Grand Central access and hotels/corporate offices are useful for portable service. The low Chinese-community score makes a culturally generic walk-in room less compelling.
7. **Long Island City — employer/resident hybrid test.** Strong transit, income, Asian-community and workplace signals with asks below prime Manhattan examples. The ZIP is heterogeneous; sell into named buildings/companies rather than extrapolating from the total.
8. **Downtown Brooklyn — plaza/event and workplace test.** Excellent transit, high household income and an active BID event platform help. Use a sponsored activation or private program; the direct cultural fit is weaker.
9. **Williamsburg — premium lifestyle test with a direct incumbent.** Puerh Brooklyn already offers self-guided gongfu, education and ceremonies. A test must differentiate by Chinese regional depth, beginner design or product conversion.
10. **Greenpoint — collaborator/competitive-learning market.** Kettl and Bellocq show premium education, retail and object demand. It is valuable for benchmarking and cross-category audiences, not the cleanest cultural launch.
11. **Sunset Park — second community/product test.** Strong Chinese-community proxy, legacy Ten Ren evidence, multiple afternoon/prepared-tea chains and relatively lower visible rent support testing. The lower income and education indicators require a value-sensitive offer.
12. **Jackson Heights/Elmhurst — diversity/access experiment.** Exceptional subway/zero-car signals and broad food ecosystems can support short-format tasting, but the Chinese-community proxy is uneven and live asks range from $42 to $150 per square foot.

### 5.4 Practical sequence before a lease

| Sequence | Market | Format | Question answered |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | Flushing North | Six-seat paid tasting in a compliant host venue | Can the concept outperform Fang’s clear $20 reference through curriculum, product attachment or repeat use? |

## 6. The clearest market whitespace

### Observed gaps

- **Beginner-readable Chinese regional pathways:** competitors offer strong experiences, but the public often has to choose a tea or ceremony before understanding the learning path.
- **Consistent cross-neighborhood availability:** several specialist formats have limited seats, limited hours, unclear prices or event-only calendars.
- **Experience-to-product instrumentation:** operators visibly sell both experiences and tea, but public evidence does not show conversion, reorder or lifetime value.
- **Portable bilingual cultural service:** institutions and employers have event demand, while specialist corporate pages show that tea can travel.
- **Unsweetened fast bridge product:** prepared-tea chains prove convenience, but there is room to test a fast beverage that leads toward whole-leaf Chinese tea rather than away from it.
- **Regional depth without collector intimidation:** a sequential curriculum by region, processing and brewing skill could distinguish the company from pan-Asian or broad “tea 101” offers.

### Inferred opening

The most defensible opening is:

> **A portable and reservation-led Chinese tea school that makes regional whole-leaf tea easy to enter, then converts the experience into take-home practice, repeat sessions, gifting and B2B programs.**

This is an inference, not a proven demand statement. It should be rejected if the pilot shows weak paid conversion, low product attachment or founder-dependent delivery.

## 8. Supply, permits and local operating implications

### 8.1 Lowest-risk supply path

For the first 12 weeks, buy from a compliant U.S. wholesaler/importer or use inventory whose import, label and traceability records are already established. This keeps the pilot focused on demand, service and conversion.

If the company imports directly, FDA states that imported food must meet U.S. safety and labeling rules; relevant foreign facilities must be registered, prior notice is required and importers may be subject to the Foreign Supplier Verification Programs rule. FDA can detain noncompliant shipments. ([FDA food import overview](https://www.fda.gov/importing-food-products-united-states), [FSVP guidance](https://www.fda.gov/media/118241/download?attachment=))

Local operating choices affect the license path:

- A café/tearoom serving drinks is generally a NYC food service establishment.
- Repacking bulk tea or bottling drinks can trigger New York State Agriculture and Markets food-processing rules; the state specifically includes repacking tea and bottling tea among Article 20-C examples. ([NYS food business licensing](https://agriculture.ny.gov/food-business-licensing))
- A retail shop selling only qualifying sealed products is different from a room brewing/serving drinks. Confirm the exact model with the agencies before signing a site.

### 8.2 NYC food-service baseline

NYC defines a food service establishment broadly as a place where food is given to people, sold or free, for on-site or take-away use. The current general permit page lists:

- **$280 annual permit fee** for most food service establishments;
- a supervising manager with a **Food Protection Certificate on site whenever operating**;
- proof of sales-tax authority and workers’ compensation/disability coverage as applicable;
- unannounced inspection and grading after opening.

The city states that an applicant may begin operating on the 22nd day after submitting a complete permit application even if the permit has not yet issued, subject to the stated process; this should not be treated as permission to ignore building, tax or other approvals. ([NYC Food Service Establishment Permit](https://nyc-business.nyc.gov/nycbusiness/description/food-service-establishment-permit), [opening a restaurant](https://www.nyc.gov/site/doh/business/food-operators/opening-a-restaurant.page))

### 8.3 Pop-ups and temporary service

A Temporary Food Service Establishment permit is required when making and serving food at qualifying temporary special events unless another applicable permit covers the operation. NYC currently lists a **$70 annual permit**, event-duration/frequency limits and a separate Food Protection Course fee. A compliant host’s permit does not automatically cover every outside operator or service arrangement; confirm responsibility in writing. ([NYC temporary permit](https://nyc-business.nyc.gov/nycbusiness/description/temporary-food-service-establishment-permit))

Before each pilot:

1. Give the venue a written service flow, ingredient/allergen list and equipment/power/water plan.
2. Determine which party’s permit covers preparation and service.
3. Confirm commissary/storage, potable water, handwashing, waste-water and temperature-control requirements.
4. Keep certificates, insurance and vendor approvals in the event file.
5. Do not assume a product-only market approval permits beverage preparation.

### 8.4 Sales tax and business registration

New York State says a seller must obtain a Certificate of Authority before making taxable sales and generally apply at least **20 days before** beginning taxable business. The exact tax treatment can differ between packaged tea, prepared beverages, food, classes and bundled products; map each SKU with a qualified adviser rather than applying one rule to all revenue. ([NYS selling products/services](https://www.tax.ny.gov/bus/doingbus/sell.htm), [register as a sales-tax vendor](https://www.tax.ny.gov/bus/st/register.htm))

### 8.5 Current wage and labor implication

New York City’s general minimum wage is **$17.00 per hour from January 1, 2026**. Tipped categories have separate cash-wage and credit rules; do not build a model around a tip credit without confirming the role and compliance. ([NYC wage rules](https://nyc-business.nyc.gov/nycbusiness/description/wage-regulations-in-new-york-state?mod=article_inline))

Illustrative proposed capacity check: a 90-minute class using two workers for 2.5 paid hours each after setup/close creates **$85 in wage-floor labor before payroll taxes, benefits or management**. At six paid guests that is $14.17 per seat before tea, food, venue, payment fees, acquisition or overhead. This is a planning calculation, not a disclosed competitor cost.

### 8.6 Fixed-site diligence

Before an LOI, verify:

- Certificate of Occupancy or Letter of No Objection and legal food/beverage use. NYC says a current/amended CO is needed when use, egress or occupancy changes, and no one may legally occupy until a CO/TCO is issued where required. ([NYC DOB Certificate of Occupancy](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/property-or-business-owner/certificate-of-occupancy.page))
- Venting, plumbing, hot water, sinks, electrical capacity, ADA access, bathroom requirement, refuse/pest plan and any assembly implications.
- Open DOB/health violations, landmark or façade constraints and signage approval.
- Whether rent is ground-floor-only, includes basement/upper floor, and whether taxes/CAM/utilities are additional.
- Personal guarantee, good-guy terms, assignment, permitted use, exclusivity, delivery condition and permit contingency.
- Outdoor seating separately; Dining Out NYC requires an application, license and location/size-based fees. ([Dining Out NYC](https://portal.311.nyc.gov/article/?kanumber=KA-03604))

### 9.2 Gates before considering a fixed appointment studio

All thresholds below are **proposed internal hurdles**, not industry standards.

| Gate | Proposed pass threshold | Why it matters |
|---|---:|---|
| Paid sample | **160+ unique paid guests** across **24+ public sessions** and at least four neighborhoods | Reduces dependence on one launch or one block |
| Repeatable fill | Last eight comparable public sessions average **60%+ paid capacity**, excluding comps | Tests continuing demand |
| Direct contribution | Core offer averages **$18+ per paid guest** after tea/food, direct venue, event labor, payment and attributable acquisition costs | Prevents revenue-only optimism |
| Product attachment | **25%+** of paid guests buy a take-home SKU at the event | Tests the hybrid thesis |
| Early repeat | **15%+** of eligible early-cohort customers make a second paid purchase within 60 days | Tests behavior beyond satisfaction |
| B2B | **4+ paid private events** from at least three independent buyers, with two repeat bookings or signed next-event commitments | Tests portable demand |
| Founder independence | Four sessions led by a trained host meet recipe, timing, story and customer-rating standards | Tests operational transfer |
| Acquisition | At least two channels produce first-60-day contribution greater than attributed acquisition/partner cost | Avoids relying on founder network or unpriced attention |

### 9.3 Additional lease gate

Do not consider a permanent public room until the company also has:

- at least six months of repeated demand, including slow-season evidence;
- a site-specific permit/build-out review by qualified local professionals;
- a conservative capacity model that covers fixed costs at the lower observed demand case;
- at least nine months of post-build-out cash runway under the company’s own downside model;
- proposed occupancy cost no greater than **15% of conservative net sales** in that model;
- a tested plan for revenue outside public seat-hours;
- no unresolved uncertainty about legal use, plumbing, accessibility, venting or food-service responsibility.

The 15% occupancy threshold is a proposed risk limit for this project, not an asserted restaurant benchmark.

### 9.4 Required event-level data

For every activation record:

- offer, date, neighborhood, venue and partner;
- seats offered, paid, comped, no-show and waitlisted;
- price paid, discount source and acquisition channel;
- party size, local/visitor self-report and first/repeat status;
- start/end, setup/close and labor minutes by role;
- grams of each tea, food, disposables, breakage and waste;
- venue, permit, insurance allocation, transport, payment and ad/partner cost;
- product units, margin and customer-level attachment;
- consented lead, 30/60/90-day reorder and referred purchase;
- refund, complaint, allergen/safety incident and qualitative reason for not buying.

Report contribution after direct costs separately from gross sales. Do not mix sponsor-paid attendance, free library programming and consumer-paid demand.

## 12. Bottom line

New York is a strong **validation** market because it combines large Chinese-community districts, global visitor and business-event channels, dense transit, major workplace clusters and a visible paid tea-education ladder.

It is also a dangerous **lease-first** market. High-skill specialists, free tastings, prepared-tea chains, premium adjacent brands, high wages, wide rent dispersion and multiple physical retreats mean that customer interest in tea is not enough.

The next decision is not “Which neighborhood should get the store?” It is:

> **Can a portable, beginner-readable Chinese tea curriculum produce repeatable contribution, product reorder and B2B demand across at least two New York demand systems without depending on founder labor?**

Run Flushing, downtown Manhattan, a workplace corridor and Sunset Park as comparable paid tests. Lease only if the behavior, unit economics and delivery system pass the proposed gates.

## Appendix A. Neighborhood scorecard methodology and evidence

### A1. Exact scoring method

The score uses seven 0-100 component indices.

1. **Cultural fit**
   - 50% Chinese-alone proxy, normalized from 0% to 60%.
   - 50% population age 5+ speaking Chinese at home, normalized from 0% to 56%.
2. **Resident purchasing**
   - 55% median household income, normalized from $45,000 to $175,000.
   - 45% bachelor’s degree or higher among age 25+, normalized from 20% to 90%.
3. **Daytime demand**
   - 65% natural log of 2023 LODES primary workplace jobs, normalized from 10,000 to 250,000.
   - 35% jobs/resident ratio, normalized from 0.2 to 6.0.
4. **Access**
   - 60% ACS access: 60% transit commute share normalized 25%-65%, plus 40% zero-vehicle household share normalized 40%-85%.
   - 40% documented-network judgment: 72-100 according to direct subway/LIRR coverage and transfer depth. This judgment is shown below.
5. **Commercial ecosystem**
   - 60% 2023 ZIP Business Patterns NAICS 722 establishments per 10,000 residents, normalized from 20 to 200.
   - 40% NAICS 722515 establishments per 10,000, normalized from 3 to 35.
6. **Cost**
   - Inverse of the dated representative asking-rent input, normalized from $30 to $160 per square foot per year.
   - The citywide $17 wage floor is not scored because it is common to every row.
7. **Testability**
   - Analyst score 75-95 based on visible host institutions, event platforms, current bookable tea formats and ability to test without a lease.

Values outside a normalization range are capped at 0 or 100. For multi-ZIP markets, component scores are the unweighted arithmetic mean of the component-ZCTA scores; this is **not** a combined ACS estimate.

### B4. Score reproduction

For any metric x normalized between lower L and upper U:

score = max(0, min(100, 100 × (x - L) / (U - L)))

Cost reverses the direction:

cost score = max(0, min(100, 100 × (160 - asking rent) / 130))

Apply component formulas and format weights in Appendix A1. For a multi-ZIP market, calculate each ZIP component first, then take the simple mean of those ZIP component scores.

## Appendix C. Source, status and model cautions

- **Cutoff:** August 28, 2026. Recheck booking pages, hours, chain units and listings immediately before fieldwork.
- **Primary-source priority:** operator, government and institutional pages are used wherever practical. Secondary sources appear mainly for physical closures or conflicting status.
- **No private revenue estimates:** the report does not infer competitor sales from reviews, traffic or longevity.
- **Asking rents:** visible asks can be withdrawn, negotiated or exclude large additional costs.
- **ZCTA geography:** postal statistical areas do not equal neighborhood boundaries or walksheds.
- **ACS sampling:** five-year estimates blend 2020-2024 and have sampling error; medians cannot be correctly population-weighted across ZCTAs.
- **Cultural proxies:** the Chinese-alone and language fields are narrow public-data proxies, not a definition of customer identity or demand.
- **LODES:** workplace primary jobs are not visits or occupied seats; self-employment and other categories are not fully represented.
- **CBP:** NAICS 722515 is a broad snack/nonalcoholic-beverage category, not a competitor count.
- **Access:** station availability does not measure a candidate door’s pedestrian flow, accessibility outages or perceived comfort.
- **Scores:** cut points, weights and analyst judgments are transparent but subjective. Use sensitivity analysis before narrowing sites.
- **Permits:** public summaries are not legal advice. Exact menus, preparation, repacking, venue contracts and premises determine requirements.
- **Market claims:** a current operator, full library event or large tourism total demonstrates a channel or behavior, not sufficient demand for this company.
